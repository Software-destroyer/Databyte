Our project addresses a critical cybersecurity question: why machine learning
models that achieve 99% accuracy in research fail when deployed in real networks."

[SLIDE 2: 1. Introduction — i. Background of the Study]
"Network Intrusion Detection Systems (NIDS) are the frontline defense inspecting
network traffic in real time for exploits, protocol violations, and data exfiltration.

Historically, signature-based systems like Snort, Suricata, and Zeek were used.
While precise on known threats, they are fundamentally blind to novel zero-day attacks.

To overcome this, cybersecurity shifted to Machine Learning and Deep Learning.
Models like Decision Trees, Random Forests, SVM, and XGBoost learn statistical
patterns from flow records, routinely reporting 98% to 99.5% accuracy on benchmarks
like CICIDS2017 and UNSW-NB15."

[SLIDE 3: ii. Problem Statement — The Same-Dataset Evaluation Illusion]
"However, this near-perfect accuracy hides a major flaw: the 'Same-Dataset
Evaluation Illusion.'

In the laboratory, models are trained and tested on splits from the same dataset.
They share the exact same network topology, background OS, and tooling — a closed world.
The model memorizes simulator artifacts rather than real attack patterns.

When deployed into a different network — the cross-dataset setting — the model faces
covariate shift and concept drift.

As shown on the slide, accuracy plummets from 99% down toward random guessing, and
the Matthews Correlation Coefficient collapses toward zero.

I now hand over to Tehjib Almas Junaid to discuss the scope and literature review."

[SLIDE 4: iii. Importance & iv. Scope of the Study]
"Deployed intrusion detection systems that fail under real-world drift create false
confidence while leaving networks exposed. Diagnosing and closing this gap is
essential for trustworthy, deployable ML security.

Our project scope is defined across four clear areas:
1. Two Benchmark Datasets: CICIDS2017 with 80+ features, and UNSW-NB15 with 49 features.
2. Five Classifier Families: Decision Tree, Random Forest, Logistic Regression, SVM,
   and XGBoost.
3. Bidirectional Transfer: Testing both CICIDS to UNSW, and UNSW to CICIDS.
4. Active Mitigation Layer: Engineering feature selection, robust normalization, and
   CORAL covariance alignment."

[SLIDE 5: 2. Literature Review (Part 1)]
"We critically reviewed five foundational papers in cross-dataset NIDS:

First, Verkerken et al. (2022) in the Journal of Network and Systems Management
evaluated bidirectional transfer between CICIDS2017 and UNSW-NB15. They proved
that accuracy plummets by 28% to 42%, concluding that feature harmonization is mandatory.

Second, Cantone et al. (2024) at IEEE CSR tested pairwise transfer across four
CIC-family datasets, showing that cross-dataset MCC collapses from over 99.6% down to
near-zero (~40% best case).

Third, Ahmim et al. (2024) evaluated UNSW-NB15 and TON_IoT, finding that using a naive
intersection of only 7 features caused accuracy to decline by up to 35%."

[SLIDE 6: 2. Literature Review (Part 2)]
"Continuing our literature synthesis:

Fourth, Bertoli et al. (2022) trained DoS-detection models on CICIDS2017 and tested
them on an independent physical testbed, documenting a 25.63% degradation because
models rely on non-transferable background features.

Fifth, Sarhan et al. (2021) explored standardized NetFlow feature extraction across
four benchmarks, confirming that feature heterogeneity is the primary obstacle to
transferability.

I now invite Bamelaai Marbaniang to present the research gaps, feasibility, and objectives."

[SLIDE 7: 3. Research Gap — Three Pillars]
"Synthesizing the reviewed literature reveals three critical research gaps:

Pillar I: 'Diagnosis vs. Remediation.' Previous studies — including Verkerken et al.
and Cantone et al. — observed the collapse and stopped. They treated it as an
observational dead-end. Our project moves from observational diagnosis to active
engineering remediation. We actively engineer solutions to bridge the gap!

Pillar II: 'Semantic Feature Ontology Mismatch.' CICIDS uses CICFlowMeter (80+ features)
while UNSW uses Argus/Zeek (49 features). No standardized semantic alignment exists
between them.

Pillar III: 'No Bidirectional Robustness Benchmark.' Most studies test only one
direction and never systematically compare whether tree-based models or margin-based
SVMs are more resilient to domain shift."

[SLIDE 8: 4. Feasibility Study]
"We conducted a four-dimension feasibility study:

1. Technical: Datasets are publicly hosted with zero licensing restrictions.
   Algorithms are supported by Scikit-Learn and XGBoost. Memory is constrained
   under 6 GB RAM using stratified subsampling (200k–500k records).

2. Economic: The entire software stack is open-source. Experiments run on our laptops
   and free Google Colab GPUs. The total budget is ₹0.00.

3. Operational: Divided into 4 modular engineering packages mapped directly to our
   4 team members, coordinated via GitHub.

4. Schedule & Risk: Planned across an 8–10 week timeline. Class imbalance is handled
   via stratified sampling and SMOTE-NC; feature scales via Robust and Quantile scaling."

[SLIDE 9: 5. Objectives]
"Based on these gaps, our project has four clear objectives:
1. Analyze and mathematically formulate why single-dataset validation fails.
2. Design a bidirectional testbed harmonizing CICIDS2017 and UNSW-NB15 into a shared,
   semantically aligned feature space.
3. Benchmark five classifiers on same-dataset and cross-dataset regimes using Accuracy,
   Precision, Recall, Macro-F1, MCC, and False Alarm Rate.
4. Apply feature selection and CORAL domain-alignment to reduce the generalization gap,
   and deploy an interactive Streamlit dashboard.

I now invite Md Sabique Huda to walk through our methodology, expected outcomes, and timeline."

[SLIDE 10: 6. Methodology / Planning of Work]
"Our methodology follows an end-to-end 7-step pipeline:
  • Step 1: Dataset Acquisition & Preprocessing — cleaning infinite/NaN values.
  • Step 2: Semantic Feature Mapping — linking equivalent features (duration, packet rates).
  • Step 3: Same-Dataset Baselines — establishing upper-bound ceilings (97%–99%).
  • Step 4: Bidirectional Cross-Dataset Transfer — evaluating CICIDS → UNSW and UNSW → CICIDS.
  • Step 5: Feature Selection & Domain Alignment — applying Mutual Information and CORAL
    covariance alignment without requiring target labels.
  • Step 6: Retrain & Evaluate — calculating the Generalization Recovery Ratio (GRR).
  • Step 7: Streamlit Dashboard — creating a live security demonstrator.

As shown in our 9-week timeline table:
  • Weeks 1–2: Acquisition & analysis (Pynskhemlang)
  • Weeks 2–4: Ontology & baseline models (Pynskhemlang, Tehjib)
  • Weeks 4–6: Cross-dataset evaluation (Tehjib, Sabique)
  • Weeks 6–7: Domain alignment mitigations (Bamelaai)
  • Weeks 7–9: Streamlit dashboard, validation & final report (All members)."

[SLIDE 11: 7. Expected Outcome]
"Our project delivers five concrete deliverables:
1. A Bidirectional Evaluation Harness: An automated Python pipeline for cross-dataset testing.
2. An Empirical Generalization Map: Quantitative proof of performance collapse across 5 models.
3. A Feature Alignment Toolkit: Reconciling CICFlowMeter and Argus/Zeek schemas with CORAL.
4. A Classifier Robustness Ranking: Empirical ranking of which model architectures best
   withstand covariate shift.
5. An Interactive Streamlit Dashboard: A production-ready web app allowing security analysts
   to simulate cross-dataset transfer and visualize confusion matrices in real time."
