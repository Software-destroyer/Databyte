────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────
[SLIDE CONTENT]:

  - Signature-based systems (Snort, Suricata, Zeek) — blind to zero-days
  - ML/DL approaches (Decision Trees, Random Forests, SVM, XGBoost) — report 98%–99.5%

[SPEECH]:
"Network Intrusion Detection Systems (NIDS) are the frontline defense inspecting
network traffic in real time for malicious exploits, policy violations, and data
exfiltration.

Traditionally, networks relied on signature-based systems such as Snort, Suricata,
and Zeek. While they have high precision on known attacks, they are fundamentally
blind to zero-day exploits, evasive polymorphic malware, or slow reconnaissance.

To overcome this, cybersecurity researchers shifted to Machine Learning and Deep
Learning. Algorithms such as Decision Trees, Random Forests, Support Vector
Machines, and XGBoost learn statistical flow patterns from network packets.
In published academic papers, these models routinely report 98% to 99.5% accuracy
on standard benchmark datasets like CICIDS2017 and UNSW-NB15.

However, as we will see on the next slide, this near-perfect accuracy hides a
dangerous operational illusion."

SLIDE 3: ii. Problem Statement — The Same-Dataset Evaluation Illusion
────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────
[SLIDE CONTENT]:
  - In the Lab: Models trained & tested on same dataset (80/20 splits, k-fold CV).
    Share identical network topology, tools, hosts, time windows — closed world.
    Models memorize simulator artifacts, not real attack signatures.
  - In Deployment: Moved to a different network (cross-dataset), face covariate
    shift & concept drift.
  - Collapse: Accuracy plummets from ~99% toward random guessing; MCC collapses to near-zero.
  - Stat: 99% → near-random.

[SPEECH]:
"Here is the problem statement at the heart of our project.

In academic laboratory setups, models are trained and tested on splits from the
EXACT SAME dataset — typically using an 80/20 partition or k-fold cross-validation.
Because training and test data share the exact same network topology, background
operating systems, packet generation tooling, and capture time window, the model
is operating in a 'closed world.' Instead of learning generalizable attack
signatures, the model memorizes simulator-specific artifacts.

When that model is actually deployed into a different network — what we call the
cross-dataset setting — it encounters severe covariate shift and concept drift.

As a result, the accuracy plummets from 99% down toward random guessing (40% to 60%),
and the Matthews Correlation Coefficient (MCC) collapses toward zero. A system that
appeared foolproof in the lab completely fails in production."

SLIDE 5: 2. Literature Review (Part 1)
────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────
[SLIDE CONTENT TABLE]:
  1. Verkerken et al. (2022) | Bidirectional cross-dataset evaluation, CICIDS2017 ↔ UNSW-NB15, multiple ML models | Cross-dataset transfer causes accuracy to plummet 28–42%; feature harmonization is mandatory
  2. Cantone, Marrocco & Bria (2024) | 4 classifiers trained/tested pairwise across 4 CIC-family datasets | Intra-dataset MCC >99.6%; cross-dataset MCC collapses to near-zero (~40% best case)
  3. Ahmim, Amangele & Moustafa (2024) | Cross-dataset experiments on UNSW-NB15 & TON_IoT with 7 shared features | Accuracy declines up to 35%; naive feature intersection fails to preserve detection

[SPEECH]:
"To place our work in context, we reviewed the key literature on cross-dataset NIDS:

First, Verkerken et al. (2022) in the Journal of Network & Systems Management
performed bidirectional cross-dataset evaluation between CICIDS2017 and UNSW-NB15.
They proved that accuracy plummets by 28% to 42%, and they concluded that feature
harmonization is mandatory.

Second, Cantone et al. (2024) at IEEE CSR tested 4 classifiers across 4 datasets in
the CIC family. They demonstrated that while intra-dataset MCC exceeds 99.6%, cross-
dataset MCC collapses to near-zero (only ~40% in the best case).

Third, Ahmim et al. (2024) evaluated UNSW-NB15 and TON_IoT, but restricted their
analysis to a naive intersection of only 7 features, suffering an accuracy decline of up
to 35% and proving that crude feature intersection alone is insufficient."

SLIDE 7: 3. Research Gap — Three Pillars
────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────
[SLIDE CONTENT]:
  - Pillar I: Diagnosis vs. Remediation — Existing studies diagnose the collapse
    but treat it as an observational dead-end rather than engineering active remediation.
  - Pillar II: Semantic Feature Ontology Mismatch — CICIDS2017 (CICFlowMeter) and
    UNSW-NB15 (Argus/Zeek) use incompatible naming, scales, and definitions — no
    standardized alignment ontology exists between them.
  - Pillar III: No Bidirectional Robustness Benchmark — Most studies test only one
    transfer direction and never systematically compare how different classifier
    families (tree-based vs. margin-based) withstand domain shift.

[SPEECH — CRITICAL DEFENSE SLIDE]:
"A rigorous synthesis of these papers reveals three critical research gaps:

Pillar I: 'Diagnosis vs. Remediation.' This directly answers why our project is
unique. Previous authors — like Verkerken et al. (2022) and Cantone et al. (2024) —
observed the collapse, documented it, and stopped. They treated it as an observational
dead-end. Our project moves from observational diagnosis to ACTIVE ENGINEERING REMEDIATION.
We don't just show that accuracy drops — we engineer methods to bring it back!

Pillar II: 'Semantic Feature Ontology Mismatch.' CICIDS uses CICFlowMeter with 80+
features; UNSW uses Argus/Zeek with 49 features. No standardized semantic ontology
currently bridges these two leading benchmark families.

Pillar III: 'No Bidirectional Robustness Benchmark.' Prior works test unidirectional
transfer and do not systematically benchmark which model architecture — tree-based
like Random Forest and XGBoost versus margin-based like SVM — is intrinsically most
resilient to domain shift."

SLIDE 10: 6. Methodology / Planning of Work
────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────
[SLIDE CONTENT]:
  - 7-Step Pipeline:
      1. Dataset Acquisition & Preprocessing
      2. Semantic Feature Mapping
      3. Same-Dataset Baselines
      4. Bidirectional Cross-Dataset Transfer
      5. Feature Selection & Domain Alignment
      6. Retrain & Evaluate (GRR)
      7. Streamlit Dashboard
  - Two symmetric transfer directions: CICIDS2017 → UNSW-NB15 and UNSW-NB15 → CICIDS2017
  - Timeline & Team Table:
      • Weeks 1–2: Acquisition & exploratory analysis (Pynskhemlang)
      • Weeks 2–4: Feature ontology & baseline models (Pynskhemlang, Tehjib)
      • Weeks 4–6: Bidirectional evaluation & diagnosis (Tehjib, Sabique)
      • Weeks 6–7: Feature selection & domain alignment (Bamelaai)
      • Weeks 7–9: Streamlit dashboard, validation & report (All members)

[SPEECH]:
"Our research methodology follows an end-to-end 7-step pipeline:
  • Step 1: Ingest official CSV records, cap infinite flow rates, and clean missing values.
  • Step 2: Build the semantic mapping dictionary linking equivalent features
    (e.g., CICIDS 'Flow Duration' ↔ UNSW 'dur'; 'Flow Packets/s' ↔ 'rate').
  • Step 3: Establish the same-dataset performance ceiling (expected 97%–99%).
  • Step 4: Execute zero-shot bidirectional cross-dataset transfer (CICIDS → UNSW and
    UNSW → CICIDS), exposing the performance drop.
  • Step 5: Apply Mutual Information feature selection, Quantile transformation, and
    Correlation Alignment (CORAL) to align feature covariance matrices without target labels.
  • Step 6: Retrain models on aligned features and calculate the Generalization Recovery
    Ratio (GRR) to measure how much lost performance was recovered.
  • Step 7: Package the pipeline into an interactive Streamlit web dashboard.

As shown in our 9-week timeline, each phase is assigned to specific team members to
ensure smooth and timely execution."

Q1: "In Slide 5, you cite Verkerken et al. (2022) who already tested CICIDS2017 ↔ UNSW-NB15.
     How is your work different or new?"
A1: "Sir/Madam, Verkerken et al. (2022) conducted an observational study. They observed that
     accuracy drops by 28–42% and noted that 'feature harmonization is mandatory', but they
     did NOT implement an active solution. They left it as an open question.
     Our project represents Research Gap I: Diagnosis vs. Remediation (Slide 7). We actively
     construct the semantic ontology, apply CORAL covariance alignment, evaluate the Generalization
     Recovery Ratio (GRR), and deploy an interactive Streamlit tool. Verkerken diagnosed the problem;
     we engineer the remedy."

Q2: "In Slide 4, why did you choose CICIDS2017 and UNSW-NB15 specifically?"
A2: "Because they represent the two most influential but structurally incompatible benchmark
     families globally. CICIDS was captured on a 1 Gbps network using CICFlowMeter (80+ features),
     whereas UNSW was captured on a 10 Gbps testbed using Argus/Zeek (49 features). Testing transfer
     between them represents the most authentic simulation of deploying a model into an unseen network."

Q3: "In Slide 10, how does CORAL alignment work without target labels?"
A3: "CORAL is unsupervised. It calculates the covariance matrix of the source traffic and the
     target traffic. It whitens the source features and recolors them using the target covariance.
     Because it only needs traffic distributions and NOT labels, it is 100% practical for real-world
     deployment where incoming target network traffic is unlabeled."
